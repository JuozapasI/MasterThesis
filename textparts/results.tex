\section{Intergenic regions}

Fourteen datasets from various tissues (blood, brain, eye, and lung) were analyzed.
These datasets contained between 11\% and 29\% unassigned uniquely mapped reads
(the table with statistics can be found in Appendix \ref{tab:countStats}) when using standard reference (taken from 10x Genomics).
The majority of those unassigned reads were intergenic, meaning they did not overlap with any reference used.
Given the dataset sizes, this proportion of reads could potentially contain biologically relevant information.

\paragraph{Analysing intergenic regions}

For each sample, intergenic regions containing a sufficient number of unassigned reads were identified,
as described in the methods section (with a threshold set at 5 CPM).
The term \textit{intergenic} is used here in a strand-specific manner,
meaning an intergenic region may be located on the opposite strand of a known gene.

To determine whether these intergenic regions contain biologically meaningful information,
cells in each sample were clustered based solely on reads from these regions.
As shown in selected examples in Figure \ref{fig:intergenicClustering},
clustering was observed and roughly corresponded to clustering based on the standard (\textit{10x Genomics}) annotation.
This indicates that biologically meaningful information is indeed present in these intergenic regions.
Consequently, further analyses were performed on these regions.

The lists of intergenic regions from all samples were combined and filtered based on the number of samples in which they were detected,
resulting in a final set of 2,590 intergenic regions.
Of these, 147 did not overlap known genes on the opposite strand and will be referred to as \textit{isolated}.
Those located on the opposite strand of known genes will be referred to as \textit{antisense}.

\paragraph{A-rich sequences near the intergenic regions 3' end}

If there is a poly-A region near the 3' end of the intergenic region, then the presence of those peaks can be explained by mis-priming,
i.e. that would suggest that our intergenic region is not necessarily the actual 3' end of the novel transcript, but may be in the middle of it.

This is indeed the case for most of the reported intergenic regions,
see Figure \ref{fig:poly-A} for visual example and some statistics.
The poly-A region here is defined as a region with at least 10 consecutive A's, allowing one mismatch.

\paragraph{Correlations}

Another aspect to assess is whether expression of those intergenic regions correlate with the expression of nearby genes.
If so, this may be due to several factors:
\begin{itemize}
  \item If the region correlates with gene on the same strand:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Technical issues – the genome annotation might be not comprehensive and not include some longer transcripts
    (that would span intergenic region as well as the gene with which correlation is observed).
    \item Biological reasons – genes in close proximity tend to be coexpressed due to the shared chromatin structure.
  \end{itemize}
  \item If the region correlates with gene on the oposite strand:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Technical artefacts – errors during library preparation could produce inversed cDNA (will be discussed later).
    \item Biological reasons – as mentioned before, genes in close proximity tend to be coexpressed.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

Which of these is the case in our samples, is hard to tell.
Out of all defined intergenic regions, 97 (3.74 \%) showed Spearman correlation \textgreater 0.5 (p \textless 0.05) in at least 1 sample
(83 correlated with genes on the opposite strand, 19 with a gene on the same strand, 5 with both).
Some examples can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:correlationScatterplots}.

In the following subsections isolated and antisense intergenic regions will be discussed separatelly.

\subsection{Isolated intergenic regions}

The intergenic regions that are distant from known genes and contain reads may indicate novel genes.
To determine whether these are genuine signals rather than artifacts, several aspects were considered:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Differential expression: Are these regions differentially expressed in any samples, i.e., are they specific to certain cell types?
  If so, this strongly suggests a biological origin rather than an artifact.
  \item Open chromatin: Are open chromatin regions present upstream? Such regions often indicate active transcription.
  \item Conservation score: Coding regions tend to be more evolutionarily conserved than non-coding regions.
  \item Gene predictions: Do these regions overlap with predicted genes identified by computational tools?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Differential expression analysis}

To assess differential expression, all data samples were first clustered.
For \textit{PBMC\_10x} samples, clustering and cell annotation were performed using \textit{CellTypist}.
For other samples, the Leiden algorithm was used, with manually chosen parameters to ensure a comparable number of clusters across similar datasets.
For instance, all \textit{lung} samples were clustered into five groups, aligning approximately with visible UMAP structures.

Regions were filtered for differential expression using thresholds of 0.01 for adjusted p-value and 0.5 for log fold change.
In total, 29 isolated intergenic regions met these criteria.
Some examples can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:isolatedDGE}.

\paragraph{Open chromatin}

Open chromatin regions may provide further evidence of transcriptional activity when located upstream.
However, distances to the nearest known genes must also be considered,
as upstream open chromatin sites may not be associated with the intergenic regions but rather with other genes.

For each intergenic region, distances to the nearest open chromatin sites and upstream genes (strand-unspecific) were calculated.
Of the 147 isolated intergenic regions, 66 had open chromatin regions closer than any known upstream genes.
To ensure that these sites were not merely associated with distant genes, an additional filter was applied, 
requiring that the closest open chromatin region be at least 2,000 bp from the nearest known gene.
This yielded 40 intergenic regions.

It should be noted that PBMC ATAC data was used for open chromatin regions,
thus this feature should be taken with precaution for regions derived from other datasets.

\paragraph{Conservation scores}

The conservation score shows evolutionary conservation and is computed comparing genomic sequences from different species.
A high conservation score in an intergenic region suggests potential functionality, providing additional evidence for biological relevance.
To identify such regions, those with conservation scores above 0.6 were defined as conserved (there were 5 of such isolated regions).
The overall conservation scores were quite low, suggesting that if the isolated intergenic genes originate from unannotated genes,
they are probably human specific.
The histogram showing conservation scores in isolated regions is given in the Figure \ref{fig:conservations}.

\paragraph{Gene predictions}

To assess whether isolated intergenic regions overlap with predicted genes, UCSC gene prediction archives were examined,
focusing on overlaps with 3' ends of predicted genes.

Of the identified regions, 12 overlapped with predicted genes.
However, three of these did not overlap with the 3' ends of predicted genes,
which would be expected given that the data was generated using 3' end sequencing methods.
Five predicted genes were extensions of known genes,
while the remaining four corresponded to genes absent from RefSeq and NCBI references but supported by our data.
Table summarizing these findings can be found in Appendix \ref{tab:predictedIsolated}.

\paragraph{Combining supporting features}

In total, four features were examined as supportive evidence for the biological significance of these intergenic regions:
conservation, open chromatin presence, differential expression, and gene predictions.
Based on these, four sublists of isolated intergenic regions were generated:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Conserved regions
  \item Regions associated with open chromatin
  \item Differentially expressed regions
  \item Regions overlapping predicted genes
\end{itemize}

Notably, the list of conserved regions had only one element (INT1216) in common with the other lists (particularly, prediction list).
The predicted gene list had two regions in common with the differential expression list (INT196, INT1525)
and one in common with the open chromatin list (INT4147).
All three of these predicted genes were extended versions of already annotated genes (see example in Figure \ref{fig:predictedExtension}).
The differential expression and open chromatin lists had 11 regions in common
(INT1312, INT1426, INT1492, INT1554, INT1609, INT1993, INT2209, INT349, INT368, INT4216, INT6013).
No regions appeared in more than two lists.

\subsection{Antisense intergenic regions}

Interpreting antisense intergenic regions is more complex.
The features such as open chromatin, AT-richness or conservativeness are not strand specific,
meaning that if they would be present, they could not be attributed with certainty for our intergenic regions.
The differential expression can be checked, however, even if the antisense intergenic region is differentially expressed,
it could still be an artifact, originating from differentially expressed transcripts.
Hence from the features checked for the isolated intergenic regions, only real supporting evidence would be computational gene prediction.

\paragraph{Gene predictions}

As in the case of isolated intergenic regions,
antisense intergenic regions were checked for overlaps with predicted genes from UCSC prediction archive.
There were 49 such regions found, out of them 35 were in the 3' ends of predicted genes.
9 of those 35 predicted genes were longer version of already annotated genes in RefSeq or GENCODE references.
The all list can be found in the Appendix \ref{tab:predictionsAntisense}.

\paragraph{Poly-A segments}

Having in mind that majority of the antisense intergenic regions have poly-A stretch after 3' end,
and some of them strongly correlates with genes on the opposite strands,
it is possible that they are artefacts from the library preparation.
Particularly, it could happen that poly-T primer binds to poly-A stretch of cDNA produced by other primer,
and in such way inversed transcripts are produced.
These are later mapped to antisense strands of known genes (see Figure \ref{fig:mispriming}).


\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{images/mispriming.jpg}
  \caption{Scheme of poly-A mispriming resulting in reads mapped to the opposite strand compared to the original gene.
  a) RNA is captured by a poly-A tail with a primer containing a poly-T sequence.
  b) Reverse transcriptase attaches to the primer and synthesizes cDNA from the RNA template.
  c) If the RNA-cDNA complex becomes unstable and dissociates, another primer may bind to the poly-A stretches on the cDNA,
  if present. This would result in reversed cDNA, causing the reads to be mapped to the antisense strand.}
  \label{fig:mispriming}
\end{figure}

\section{Enhancing transcriptomic reference}

Keeping in mind that unassigned reads are also present in gene locations,
we attempted to resolve overlapping genes in the annotations
so that reads from these regions could be included in the downstream analysis.
For overlap resolution, a simple set of rules was applied,
primarily favoring protein-coding genes over others and considering the level field in the transcriptomic annotations
(more details can be found in Methods \ref{sec:overlappers}).

In addition to resolving overlaps, the initial transcriptomic reference (taken from 10x Genomics)
was enriched with entries from other transcriptomic references (RefSeq, GENCODE, lncRNApedia),
ensuring that new entries did not overlap those already present.
Finally, gene entries with reads near their 3' ends were extended.

This enhanced reference resulted in an average increase of 1.2\% more reads in the cell-gene matrices.
While this increase is relatively small, it enabled the detection of approximately 350 additional genes
that were undetected using the standard transcriptomic reference (an example is given in Figure \ref{fig:overlapResolved}).
%However, an average of 13 genes per dataset were lost when using this enhanced reference.

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/TYMP.png}
  \caption{Example of successful resolution of overlapping genes TYMP and SCO2.
  Both genes are protein coding, however TYMP is preffered since it has lower \textit{level} value.
  Based on the exon structures, it can clearly be seen that those reads in the overlapping area indeed originate from TYMP gene.
  Without reference enhancing, these reads from overlapping area would be classified as multimappers, and filtered out (in typical workflow).
  The 10x annotation track is expanded to highlight exon structures.}
  \label{fig:overlapResolved}
\end{figure}

Although the overall data volume increased, it is not straightforward to conclude that data quality improved.
Overlap-handling rules can be debated, particularly regarding their applicability in all cases.

For example, genes are expressed in certain cell types or states regardless of their level classification
(whether verified, manually annotated, or automatically annotated).
While, in general, verified or manually annotated genes are more frequently expressed than automatically annotated ones
(which are likely less explored and detected at lower rates overall),
this pattern does not hold in all situations.
The same consideration applies to gene classification by type.

Regarding the gene extensions, in most cases they increase the gene expression by relatively small fraction of counts
(see Figure \ref{fig:extension} for example).
Even though some additional information is gained, when compared with the total amount of data, this gain is very small
(e.g., in \textit{PBMC\_10x\_2} sample, it constituted only 1.76\% of gained reads, or 0.017\% of total reads).

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/extension_FBX042.png}
  \caption{Example of gene extension (coverage shown is from \textit{PBMC\_10x\_2} dataset).
  In this case gene FBX042 is extended to include reads close to its 3' end.
  However, the amount of reads gained is tiny when compared with the number of total counts of this gene
  (the number of counts of this gene increased approximatelly by 3\% in the \textit{PBMC\_10x\_2} dataset).}
  \label{fig:extension}
\end{figure}

Overall, while statistical differences are evident,
their impact on biological interpretations remains uncertain and likely depends on the specific downstream analysis.
For instance, cell clustering is generally unaffected, and a visual inspection of UMAPs
does not reveal significant differences between datasets generated using the enhanced and standard references
(see example in Figure \ref{fig:enhancedClustering}).
However, in certain cases, even a small number of additional genes could significantly influence the analysis,
meaning that this enhanced reference could help uncover relevant biological processes.

\section{Unmapped reads}

In the previous sections, mapped but unassigned reads were explored.
However, in scRNA-seq data, there are also reads that do not map to the reference genome at all.
In the analyzed datasets, such reads constituted approximately 3–13\% (mean 5.96\%) of total reads.
The majority of these are likely technical artifacts.
Nonetheless, it is worth investigating whether some may represent meaningful sequences originating from non-human DNA, such as viral genomes.

To explore this, unmapped reads were aligned to viral genomes using the BLAST tool.
Indeed, a small fraction (around 1–2\% of unmapped reads) mapped to viral genomes.
The viral species to which reads mapped were consistent across samples, with most reads aligning to
\textit{BeAn 58058 virus}, \textit{Human endogenous retrovirus K113}, and \textit{Aeribacillus phage AP45}.

While it is difficult to draw definitive conclusions from this type of mapping,
these findings suggest that scRNA-seq data is sufficiently sensitive to detect non-host transcripts even in healthy individuals.
