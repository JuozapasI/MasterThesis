Single-cell RNA sequencing is a widely used tool for analyzing cellular systems nowadays.
These experiments generate vast amounts of data, and since they are becoming increasingly affordable and accessible,
the data is often used solely for the primary goal of the study, without deeper exploration.
One underexamined aspect in most studies is the presence of reads that map to the genome but are not assigned to any gene.
As shown in the Results chapter, such reads can comprise 10–30\% of total reads—a significant portion of the dataset.

This project aimed to investigate these unassigned reads.
They were first classified based on whether they intersected with known genes or not (referred to as intergenic).
Intergenic reads were then divided into two groups:
antisense (located on the opposite strand of known genes) and isolated (with no known genes on either strand).
Reads were clustered, and only regions with at least 5 CPM were analyzed.

\paragraph{Antisense intergenic regions}

Two key observations — expression correlations with genes on the opposite strand and poly-A stretches downstream of the regions —
led us to hypothesize that these reads may be artifacts produced during the reverse transcription step.
These reads represent the vast majority of unassigned reads,
so addressing this mechanism could improve the efficiency of single-cell RNA-seq experiments.
It should be noted that this hypothesis applies specifically to 3'-end capturing methods, which rely on poly-A tails.

Temperature adjustments might help reduce such artifacts: at lower temperatures, RNA–cDNA complexes are more stable.
In the 10x Genomics v3.1 protocol, reverse transcription is performed at $52^\circ C$, while in inDrops2 it occurs at $42^\circ C$,
where we observed fewer unassigned reads (see Appendix \ref{tab:countStats}).
However, strong conclusions cannot be drawn, as only two samples from inDrops2 were analyzed.

While this hypothesis may explain the majority of antisense reads, other possibilities should not be dismissed.
For instance, template switching — when reverse transcriptase (or polymerase) detaches and reattaches elsewhere —
is reported in both reverse transcription and amplification steps (\cite{Houseley2010, Potapov2017}).
Although artifacts from template switching are unlikely to form large clusters (due to expected more random distribution),
they may explain reads in clusters smaller than 5 CPM, which were not explored in this project.

\paragraph{Isolated intergenic regions}

Unlike antisense regions, isolated regions might represent unannotated (novel) genes rather than artifacts.
This is particularly likely for regions with supporting features, the most indicative of which is differential expression —
since noise is not expected to be cell-type specific.

Although the human genome has been extensively studied, several factors still complicate novel gene identification.
First, since protein-coding genes have more defined structures (and are thus easier to detect computationally) (\cite{Guigo2023}),
these potential novel genes are likely non-coding, such as lncRNAs.
Second, their low expression and high cell-type specificity hinder confident annotation,
which is generally required by widely used transcriptome references like GENCODE or RefSeq.

While transcript structures can be inferred using long-read RNA sequencing (as we demonstrated using Oxford Nanopore data),
high sequencing depth is needed for confident annotations.

Although lncRNAs can participate in virtually any cellular process, predicting the function of a specific lncRNA is challenging.
They often lack clear phenotypic effects, and mutations typically influence quantitative traits (\cite{Mattick2023}).
This project did not attempt to assign functions to these novel candidates; future studies could explore this.

A subset of isolated regions located near known genes could be explained by unannotated transcript extensions.
Some overlapped predicted longer isoforms of known genes, suggesting this as a possible origin.
Long-read sequencing data can be used to validate this hypothesis.

\paragraph{Enhancing transcriptomic reference}

Some reads were unassigned despite aligning to annotated gene regions.
This is mainly due to gene overlaps.
To incorporate such reads, the transcriptomic reference was enhanced by resolving overlaps based on gene types.
Using this enhanced reference, downstream cell–gene matrices increased by $\sim 1.2$\% on average.

While overall assigned reads and gene detection increased, some genes showed reduced counts after overlap resolution —
highlighting the complexity of the transcriptome and the difficulty of disambiguating gene overlaps with short reads.

Our method for enhancing transcriptomic references raises several points of consideration:
first, prioritizing gene types (e.g., protein-coding over lncRNAs) can be subjective, and may not always be appropriate.
Second, our overlap resolution used only gene-level annotations (start and end coordinates),
whereas transcriptome annotations typically include exon–intron structures and isoforms.

Alternative strategies for handling multi-mapped reads exist —
such as uniform distribution or probabilistic assignment based on uniquely mapped read ratios (\cite{Francoeur2020}).
However, no consensus or gold standard currently exists, so such reads are often excluded to reduce uncertainty.

An improved approach could consider transcript-level annotations, e.g., comparing read coverage with exon structures
to infer the most likely gene of origin in overlapping regions.
Such a tool could recover more reads and improve assignment accuracy.

\paragraph{Unampped reads}

As a side investigation, unmapped reads were also briefly explored.
While the majority are likely artifacts, some may originate from non-human organisms, such as viruses.
We found that a small fraction aligned to viral species —
suggesting that even unmapped reads may hold biological value and should not be dismissed outright.

\paragraph{Limitations and discreapncies}

This project analyzed datasets generated using short-read, 3' end-capturing RNA-seq methods.
Datasets from other methods would exhibit different artifacts and require separate analysis.
These data did not allow the definition of full transcript structures, which is possible with full-length sequencing.

An interesting discrepancy is shown in Table \ref{tab:countStats}:
the proportion of unassigned reads is lower when computed from raw counts compared to demultiplexed counts.
Since demultiplexed counts represent actual molecule counts, and raw counts reflect amplification,
one would expect similar ratios — assuming equal amplification efficiency.

However, we observed a consistently higher proportion of unassigned reads in the demultiplexed data,
suggesting that unassigned reads may be amplified less efficiently.
The origin of this bias is unclear and warrants further study.
This effect is especially pronounced in datasets generated using inDrops2,
so comparisons between inDrops2 and 10x v3.1 may help uncover its cause.
